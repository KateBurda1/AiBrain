# Right, or Just Lucky?
## 10 questions to ask before you lock next year's budget

### Before you read

This paper comes from years of sitting in budget reviews. We've watched leadership teams approve plans for the year ahead, across industries, for 28 years. The same thing keeps showing up, and it isn't a shortage of effort or a shortage of data.

It's this: most plans are approved without anyone being able to say where the growth really comes from.

Budget plans like that get signed off every year. Here's how to make sure yours isn't one of them.

---

### The short version

1. **Last year plus a percentage isn't a plan.** It's a guess with a spreadsheet.
2. **A story isn't a strategy.** Most plans explain last year. Few explain next year.
3. **Activity isn't performance.** If every tactic gets done and the number doesn't move, the tactics didn't work.
4. **More data hasn't meant better decisions.** Most tools tell you what happened. Few tell you what to do next.

Then one hindsight, three foresights, and ten questions to take into your budget review.

## Insights: what we find in budget reviews

### 1. Last year plus a percentage isn't a plan

The most common growth assumption we see is last year's number, plus a percentage. Segment by segment, account by account.

In one business we worked with, the budget did exactly that for a key revenue segment. When we asked why, the answers were honest: *"This is how we've always done it."* *"We don't have data that gives us what we're looking for."* The pricing for that segment had already been negotiated. The budgeting system just didn't connect to it.

The result: that segment was overstated by **$1.2 million** before the year even started.

**The takeaway:** a growth percentage without a reason behind it isn't a forecast. It's a hope, and it's already in the budget.

### 2. A story isn't a strategy

Ask a team for their segment strategy and you'll often get a history lesson: what happened last year, why it happened, who left, what the market did.

That's a story. A strategy is the construct of how the team expects to reach the goal. It should be clear enough, and specific enough, that you could check it.

In our own data, **only 5% of marketing initiatives were focused on the most valuable customers**, and **90% of tactics weren't aligned to the overall strategy.** That's not a talent problem. It's a planning problem.

**The takeaway:** if the plan can't say how the number gets hit, it can't be held accountable when it isn't.

### 3. Activity isn't performance

SMART goals entered business planning in 1981, and teams still feel safe building plans on them. But a goal can be specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound, and still have nothing to do with revenue.

Tactics are only activity. If a team completes every tactic on the list and the financial performance doesn't change, the tactics didn't work. Completing them isn't performance. Revenue that moves because of a strategy is.

**The takeaway:** reward impact, not activity. Otherwise you'll get plenty of activity.

### 4. More data hasn't meant better decisions

Leadership teams have more dashboards, tools and reports than ever. Budget season usually adds another: a new platform, a new sales intelligence tool, a new AI promising to tell your team who to call.

Most of it tells you what already happened. We've watched teams run their best intelligence tools after the sale closed, to explain the result, not to decide the next move. Five departments bring five versions of last year to the planning table, and the plan gets negotiated instead of built.

**The takeaway:** the question isn't whether you have enough data. It's whether the data you're budgeting on tells you what to do next.

**These four are connected.** Without data that points forward, teams roll last year forward. Without a strategy, they fill the plan with activity. And the plan gets approved, because it looks like every other plan.

## Hindsight: right, or just lucky?

An asset manager overseeing many hotels reported a **16:1 return on ad spend** and called it a win.

The campaign was aimed at their least valuable customers. Against that segment, the return needed to clear **22:1** just to break even. So 16:1 was a loss, dressed as a win. The same spend aimed at a more valuable customer would have cleared the bar at 8:1.

**A lower return would have been the better outcome.**

Nothing in their systems was broken. Every number was correct. It was measured against the wrong customer, so a good result and a bad decision looked exactly the same.

That's the question worth asking about every line in next year's budget. Which of last year's results were right, and which were just lucky?

## Foresights: what the best do differently at budget time

### 1. They plan around their most profitable customers

The best teams know their most profitable segments, accounts and customers, and they plan around them. Not around the categories they've always reported on.

They also understand what those customers are trying to achieve. Not just what they buy. The offer that wins is often about the customer's business, not your product.

### 2. They link the financial goal, the strategy and the tactics

The critical link runs in one direction: **financial goal, then segment strategy, then the tactics to achieve it.** Each one answers to the one above it.

An effective strategy reads more like a formula than a story:

> We're growing revenue 14%. Five accounts get us there. In the first, we grow 10%, from $200K to $220K. In the second, we grow our share from 20% to 68%. Here are the tactics behind each.

You can check that. You can coach to it. And at the end of the year, you'll know whether it worked, and why.

### 3. They challenge the status quo, even when it's comfortable

In one plan we reviewed, a large share of the marketing budget was going to the single most expensive channel available. Why? The person running it was well respected, the buy was easy to execute, and nobody wanted to disturb a good relationship.

Every reason was about comfort. None was about revenue. The plan was approved anyway.

The best teams ask the uncomfortable question in the room, before the budget locks. Not in the post-mortem.

## 10 questions to ask before you lock next year's budget

**The top line**
1. Where does next year's growth come from, segment by segment, and what's the strategy behind each number?
2. Which of these numbers are last year plus a percentage, and which have a reason behind them?
3. Are we planning around our most profitable customers, or around the categories we've always reported on?
4. How are we re-mixing the business for next year, and do our teams have the skills to make that shift? Shifting the mix means changing behavior, and most plans fall short there.

**Sales**

5. How deep are we inside our key accounts? How many relationships do we have beyond procurement: in finance, operations, the C-suite?
6. Where is the economy growing, and do we have a plan to be there?

**Marketing**

7. What is each marketing dollar meant to move, and for which customers?
8. Is our best return aimed at our most valuable customers, or just our easiest ones?

**The whole plan**

9. Is the data we're budgeting on telling us what happened, or what to do next?
10. If every tactic in this plan gets done and the number doesn't move, what will we have learned?

## The takeaway: strategy isn't something, it's everything

Most commercial teams are planning the way they have since the 1970s, and expecting new results. That's like yelling at a rotary phone and asking Siri for directions to the grocery store. It won't work.

Ask for a new approach and the common refrain is, "We're going back to basics." Usually because nobody is sure where to go next.

There are almost no efficiencies left to squeeze out of operations. The growth has to come from how your teams create demand, who they build it with, and whether the plan behind it holds up. Status quo isn't an option.

As James Clear put it: *"You do not rise to the level of your goals. You fall to the level of your system."*

We take all the noise, the disruption and the complexity, and bring you a clear, simple path for your team to get results. **Bringing simplicity to your complexity.**
