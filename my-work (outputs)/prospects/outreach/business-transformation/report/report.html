Insights: what leaders told us

Four patterns, again and again.

01

More data has not produced more revenue.

Leaders have more tools, systems, dashboards and reports than ever. One senior executive put it plainly: more access to data than at any point in their career, and still not the revenue lift they expected.

Where the path from data to action is short, data gets used. Operations reads a review score and fixes the problem. Pricing sees demand shift and adjusts the rate. In the commercial engine, sales and marketing, that path gets murky. Without clear sight lines from insight to action, teams go back to what they have always done.

The takeaway: data is only as good as your ability to execute on it. If no one translates it into how you win customers, it gets left behind.
02

Your leaders have no time left for strategy.

Commercial leaders described their time going to financial reviews, owner and investor reporting and internal demands. Many had been stretched across more of the business with fewer people. The common refrain: we're doing more, with less time.

03

Under pressure, teams go back to 2019.

The line we heard again and again: "If we want 2019 results, we'll do what we did in 2019." It worked once, and it's familiar. But the weather has changed. Buyers changed, segments blurred, and the people charged with creating demand aren't sure how to shift. So they retreat to what worked four, or even ten, years ago.

Outside-In Thinking, starting from the customer's customer instead of your own product, feels uncomfortable because it's unfamiliar. It's also what keeps you relevant to customers, and keeps your best people. Talented people want to do the work differently.

The takeaway: don't bring yesterday's commercial approach into today and expect it to hold. Build new skills at every level, and make sure your leaders understand the new way well enough to coach it and sustain it.
04

You're measuring activity, not impact.

When organizations measure activity, they get it. In some cases the focus on activity was so strong that teams inflated it. Activity is still widely treated as a measure of effectiveness, when often it only measures how busy the team looks on the report.

The takeaway: reporting should follow the outcome and impact of the strategy. Move from activity to effectiveness, impact and speed to market.

Hindsight: why this matters for transformation
These four patterns are connected. Reporting leaves no time for new strategy. Leaders without time, or without exposure to a different approach, fall back on what they know. Activity metrics reward it. The organization drifts into the status quo, even in the middle of a transformation.

The silent saboteur.

Most transformations are built in detail for systems, structure and the operating model. The top line is treated as the result that follows. It rarely follows on its own. The four patterns sit in the handoff between the plan and the customer, the part that never makes the deck.

Revenue has to be the plan, not the result you hope for. The science and strategy come first: the revenue goal, the strategies that move it, the actions that prove it. Then the customer engagement approach.

Foresights

What the leaders who hit the top line do differently.

Volatility isn't the weather anymore. It's the climate. These three shifts separate the plans built for it from the plans built for a passing storm.

I.

They start with the customer journey, from the point of inspiration.

The old model runs marketing first, sales next, pricing last. It describes what we do to customers. The best map the journey from the moment a customer first becomes aware of a need, the point of inspiration, all the way to the sale. At each step: what's on the customer's mind, which team's play fits, and which message goes beyond the product. By the time most organizations show up, the buyer is comparing prices.

II.

They run strategy execution, not action plans.

Action plans that are really to-do lists have passed their expiration date. So have activity goals born from an operational mindset. The best decide first which revenue model gets them to optimal revenue, prioritized by profitability. Then the go-to-market strategy sales, marketing and pricing will use to get there. Leaders are accountable for the strategy. The team owns the tactics.

III.

They integrate the commercial engine, not just the org chart.

The commercial leader now belongs at the executive table. But a reporting line and a weekly meeting don't create integration. Sales, marketing and pricing working toward one revenue model, around your most valuable customers, does.
