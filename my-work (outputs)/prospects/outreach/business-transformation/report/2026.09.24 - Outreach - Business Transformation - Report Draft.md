### Before you read

This paper started as a set of listening sessions. We spent more than 45 hours in deep-dive conversations with over 30 senior leaders: operators, sales and marketing heads, owners and investors, and the teams on the front line. The sessions were run in hospitality. What they surfaced shows up in every industry we've worked in over 28 years, from pharma and medical devices to technology and enterprise services.

We asked one question in many forms: **what stands between your organization and the next level of revenue performance?**

Nobody said strategy. Nobody said data. What they described was a gap between the plan and the top line. It's the same gap most transformations fall into.

## Insights: what leaders told us

### 1. More data has not produced more revenue

Leaders have more tools, systems, dashboards and reports than ever. One senior executive put it plainly: they had more access to data than at any point in their career, and still didn't see the revenue lift they expected.

The pattern was consistent. Where the path from data to action is short and obvious, data gets used. Operations reads a review score and fixes the problem. Pricing sees demand shift and adjusts the rate. In the commercial engine, sales and marketing, that path gets murky. Without clear sight lines from insight to action, teams go back to what they have always done.

**The takeaway:** data is only as good as your ability to execute on it. If no one translates it into how the organization wins customers, it gets left behind.

### 2. Your leaders have no time left for strategy

Commercial leaders told us they spend their time on financial reviews, owner and investor reporting, and internal demands. Many had been stretched across more of the business with fewer people. The common refrain: *we're doing more, with less time.*

In one team we studied, leaders spent two hours a month reporting, before preparation time. They spent fifteen minutes a month coaching their people on strategy and skills.

**The takeaway:** when strategy loses to reporting, leaders double down on activity instead of changing how the work gets done. Scaling your leaders' capacity to set strategy and enable their teams isn't a nice-to-have. It's what future-proofs your revenue.

### 3. Under pressure, teams go back to 2019

The line we heard again and again: *"If we want 2019 results, we'll do what we did in 2019."*

It's understandable. It worked once, and it's familiar. But the weather has changed. Buyers changed, segments blurred, and the people charged with creating demand aren't sure how to shift their approach. So they retreat to what worked four, or even ten, years ago.

Outside-in thinking, starting from the customer's customer instead of your own product, feels uncomfortable because it's unfamiliar. It's also what keeps you relevant to customers, and what keeps your best people. Talented people want to do the work differently.

**The takeaway:** don't bring yesterday's commercial approach into today and expect it to hold. Build new skills at every level, and make sure your leaders understand the new way well enough to coach it and sustain it.

### 4. You're measuring activity, not impact

When organizations measure activity, they get it. In some cases the focus on activity was so strong that teams inflated it. Activity is still widely treated as a measure of effectiveness, when often it only measures how busy the team looks on the report.

**The takeaway:** reporting should follow the outcome and impact of the strategy. Move from activity to effectiveness, impact and speed to market.

**These four are connected.** Reporting leaves no time for new strategy. Leaders without time, or without exposure to a different approach, fall back on what they know. Activity metrics reward it. The organization drifts into the status quo, even in the middle of a transformation.

## Hindsight: why this matters for transformation

Most transformations are built in detail for systems, structure and the operating model. The top line is treated as the result that follows.

It rarely follows on its own. The four patterns above are why. They sit in the handoff between the plan and the customer, the part that never makes the deck. We call it **the silent saboteur.**

Revenue has to be the plan, not the result you hope for. That means the science and strategy come first: the revenue goal, the strategies that move it, the actions that prove it. Then the customer engagement approach.

---

## Foresights: what the leaders who hit the top line do differently

### 1. They start with the customer journey, from the point of inspiration

The old model runs marketing first (pull), sales next (push), and pricing last. It describes what we do *to* customers, not how we earn their attention.

The best map the customer journey from the moment a customer first becomes aware of a need, the point of inspiration, all the way to the sale. At each step they ask what's on the customer's mind, which team's play fits best, and which message goes beyond the product. By the time most organizations show up, the buyer is comparing prices. The advantage goes to whoever understood them earlier.

### 2. They run strategy execution, not action plans

Action plans that are really to-do lists have gone past their expiration date. So have activity goals born from an operational mindset.

The leaders who hit the top line decide first which revenue model gets them to optimal revenue, prioritized by profitability. Then they decide the go-to-market strategy that sales, marketing and pricing will use to get there. They hold their leaders accountable for executing the strategy, and they leave the tactics to the team.

### 3. They integrate the commercial engine, not just the org chart

The commercial leader now belongs at the executive table. But a reporting line and a weekly meeting don't create integration. Sales, marketing and pricing working toward one revenue model, around your most valuable customers, does.

## Five questions to ask before your next quarterly review

1. Is revenue a line in your transformation plan, or a result you're hoping for?
2. Which part of the plan moves the top line this quarter, and can you say why?
3. How much time did your commercial leaders spend coaching last month, compared with reporting?
4. What in your commercial playbook is still from 2019?
5. Your best quarter last year: do you know what drove it?
