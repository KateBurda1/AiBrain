# Thoughtware Before Software

**Why account-based marketing is won at the point of inspiration**

## 1. We wish we could change the name

Account-based marketing has "marketing" in the title. So everyone assumed it belonged to marketing, and marketing ran with it. Target lists, intent data, campaigns, platforms. Somewhere in the programming, the customer got lost.

It was never meant to be a marketing project. At its best, ABM is a customer-led, account-owned growth strategy. The account lead is the tip of the spear. Marketing is the amplifier. The whole team wraps around where the customer is in their own journey.

The name matters less than what it has come to mean. For most organizations, ABM now means more technology aimed at fewer logos. That's where it goes wrong.

## 2. Where ABM derails

We see the same four patterns in almost every program that stalls.

- **It becomes higher-order campaign management.** One to many, one to few, one to one. The targeting gets sharper, but the message is still built around what we want to say, not what the customer needs to hear.
- **It stays product-led.** A campaign is built around a launch. The internal calendar drives the content. The account lead inherits collateral and is asked to send it to customers they know deeply. The customer receives messaging disconnected from where they are. The relationship absorbs the damage.
- **It focuses on deployment over approach.** Which platform, which channels, how many touches. The how gets all the budget. The why and the when get none.
- **It has a blind spot at the point of inspiration,** the one moment where it matters most.

You can run a flawless one-to-one program and still be irrelevant to the customer.

## 3. The point of inspiration

Every buying journey starts before the customer can name what they need. Something shifts. A reimbursement rule changes. A competitor moves. A key account wobbles. The board asks a question nobody can answer. The customer knows they have to do something different, but they don't know what yet.

That's the point of inspiration. And no one is there.

Most suppliers show up later, when the customer is aware of products and comparing them. By then the customer has framed the problem, set the criteria and formed a view of who understands them. Gartner found that B2B buyers spend only about 17% of their total buying time meeting with potential suppliers. That time is split across every supplier they're considering. Most of the thinking happens without you.

At the point of inspiration and the informed stage that follows, the emotional reality is everything. Most teams arrive with a product answer to an emotional question.

If you can be the one who helps them make sense of the shift, before anyone is selling, you own the mindshare. And mindshare at the start becomes market share, share of wallet and lifetime value later.

## 4. Feeling decides

B2B isn't less emotional than B2C. It's more emotional. The person across the table is staking their credibility, their team and sometimes their career on the decision. When thinking is equal, feeling decides. Every single time.

Most teams map what customers think. The decision is being made in the part they aren't mapping.

Empathy isn't flat. One person carries three pressures at once:

- **Personal:** what they feel privately. Fear, frustration, self-doubt.
- **Professional:** what's at stake for their career, credibility and reputation.
- **Organizational:** what the institution absorbs. Cost, reputation, performance, competitive position.

Take a head of cardiology at a hospital:

- *Personally:* the helplessness of losing visibility the moment a patient leaves the building, and the quiet fear of being the physician whose patients keep coming back.
- *Professionally:* exposure every time a readmission lands, measured on outcomes that depend on things outside their control.
- *Organizationally:* a program that can't show it's reducing preventable deterioration, and the financial and reputational cost of avoidable readmissions.

A product brochure speaks to none of that. A team that understands all three can be relevant long before a product is on the table.

## 5. The thoughtware

Customer-led ABM runs on a sequence. It's simple to describe and hard to shortcut. Understand before you solve.

1. **The journey stage.** Point of inspiration, informed, explore, aware, validate. Where is this customer right now, and what changed since the last stage?
2. **Thinking and feeling.** Their questions in their own words, and the emotional state underneath them, across all three pressures.
3. **Focus.** What they care about at this stage, and the window they're working against.
4. **What we have.** Everything in the "closet": capabilities, insight, partners, proof. Not just products.
5. **The sweet spot.** Where what we have meets what they care about, right now.
6. **The value proposition.** Because they're focused on this, we bring that, which creates this impact on what they care about.
7. **The differentiated message.** Only now, and only if it foots back through every step above.
8. **The channel and the champion.** Who on the team delivers it, and where.

The order is the point. A message that sounds right isn't right until it traces back through the customer's journey. Teams that start at step seven write clever lines that land nowhere.

## 6. Software is the last lap

More platforms claim to do ABM today than at any point in history. Most use software to solve problems their users don't understand yet. A fool with a tool is still a fool.

The problem isn't technology. It's technology without the thinking underneath it. Look at what each layer does:

| Layer | What it is | Can you buy it? |
|---|---|---|
| Thoughtware | Who is the persona. What they're feeling at the point of inspiration. What pressures they carry. | No |
| Methodology | The customer journey, the point of inspiration, the cost of inaction, the value story | Partly |
| Engagement planning | How you show up at each stage. What you say and when. | Partly |
| Deployment | Account planning infrastructure, channels, automation, reach and scale | Yes. This is where technology lives. |

The platforms are selling deployment infrastructure. That's useful, but only if the foundation is already built. The organizations that win won't be the ones with the best technology. They'll be the ones who built the thinking the technology runs on.

Thoughtware, then methodology, then deployment. In that order.

## 7. Customer-led, team-enabled

When marketing and sales each work in their own corner, it isn't account-based anything. It's awareness marketing with a target list. Customers feel it too. Two plans aimed at the same logo are noisy, disruptive and frustrating to be on the receiving end of.

In a customer-led, team-enabled model, the account plan and the ABM plan are the same document. It starts with the customer's journey and defines what each discipline brings:

- **Marketing brings:** the persona and the emotional insight, market intelligence, the value proposition and messaging, proof points, and the channel plan.
- **The account lead brings:** the relationship, stakeholder mapping, the opportunity portfolio, solution fit, and execution.

Together they co-orchestrate: the right message, from the right person, at the right point in the customer's journey. That's the capability no algorithm can replace.

## 8. Case study: a global medical technology company

**The situation.** A global medical technology company wanted its hospital account teams across Latin America to move from product-led selling to customer-led engagement with their most important accounts. The accounts were public and private hospital systems across Argentina, Colombia and Puerto Rico, Mexico, Chile and Brazil.

**What we did.** We took the teams from a webinar on customer-led, team-enabled ABM to a multi-day, in-person workshop. Each team took one real account and built its customer journey on a poster, stage by stage:

- the persona and their three pressures
- what they were thinking and feeling at the point of inspiration
- what the account team had in its closet
- the sweet spot
- the value proposition and differentiated messages for each stage
- who on the team would deliver each one

**What the teams left with.** Each team left with a customer journey for one real account. It started at the point of inspiration, not at the product. It gave them a message for every stage and a named owner for every touch. Just as important, sales and marketing built it together, in the same room, as one account plan.

## 9. Why it pays

Owning the point of inspiration isn't a softer way to sell. It's a harder-edged way to grow. The research on firms that do ABM well bears it out:

- **Bigger deals.** Forrester found that ABM accounts carry larger average deal sizes than non-ABM accounts in every region it studied. About a third of firms saw an 11% to 20% uplift, and nearly a third saw 21% to 50%.
- **Better return.** In Momentum ITSMA and the ABM Leadership Alliance's benchmark of 279 ABM leaders, 72% said ABM delivers a higher ROI than any other type of marketing. 84% are improving pipeline growth, and 77% are seeing revenue growth.
- **Most firms haven't built it in.** The same study found only 17% of programs are fully embedded as a foundation of how the company goes to market. For most, ABM is still a program sitting next to the account plan, not inside it.

That last number is the opportunity. The gap between firms that run ABM and firms that win with it is the thoughtware.

- **Mindshare becomes market share.** The supplier who helped the customer understand the shift is the one they call when they're ready to act.
- **Market share becomes share of wallet.** A customer who feels understood brings you the next problem before it goes to tender.
- **Share of wallet becomes lifetime value.** Relevance compounds. Every stage you show up well in makes the next one easier.

The cost of not doing it is quieter but real. Every time a team arrives with a product answer to an emotional question, trust built over years takes the hit.

## 10. Ten questions to ask about your most important account

1. What shifted in this customer's world in the last six months that they haven't named out loud yet?
2. Who was in the conversation when that shift started? Were you?
3. Can you name your key persona's personal, professional and organizational pressures?
4. Does your account plan mention what the customer is feeling, or only what they're buying?
5. Are your account plan and your marketing plan the same document?
6. Does everything you send this account trace back to where they are in their journey?
7. Do you know what's in your organization's closet beyond your product?
8. Who on your team owns each stage of this customer's journey?
9. If you removed your ABM platform tomorrow, would your strategy still stand?
10. Are you measuring clicks and impressions, or share of wallet and lifetime value?

**Mostly yes:** you're running customer-led ABM. Keep going.

**Mostly no:** you're running campaigns with a target list. You're not alone, and it's fixable.
