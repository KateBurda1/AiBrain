## Step 1 — Competitive alternatives (Kate, 2026.08.29)

Kate's direct read: "We haven't found anything like Ignite. They are trying to get there."

What buyers actually do instead today:

1. **The stitched stack plus humans (the real incumbent).** BI around revenue projections, separate BI around Salesforce, and no bridge between the two. No orchestration layer. An analyst holds it together manually.
2. **A consultancy**, including Thoughtware itself. Episodic by nature, stops when the engagement stops.
3. **Revenue management systems (IDeaS, Duetto, Amadeus and similar).** Not a real alternative, but the category Ignite is most likely to be mistaken for. See the naming risk below.
4. **Do nothing.** Keep planning annually and absorb the signal-to-action lag.

Nothing in the market brings market conditions and competitive landscape in to drive alternative models.

## Step 2 — Unique attributes (Kate, 2026.08.29)

What Ignite has that no alternative does:

- **The bridge between revenue-projection BI and sales/CRM BI.** Buyers have both sides and nothing joining them.
- **An orchestration layer.** Nothing in the current stack orchestrates; the tools report.
- **Market and competitive landscape brought in to build different models**, not just internal historicals.
- **Sits on top and listens**, continuously, rather than being run as a project.
- **Produces a playbook teams can action**, which is where every reporting tool stops.

**Explicit scope boundary, stated by Kate:** Ignite does **not** do room allotments or pricing. It is not a revenue management system. It sits above that layer and works on overall revenue strategy.

## Naming risk (flagged 2026.08.29, needs a decision)

In hospitality, "revenue" is an occupied word. Said to an owner or asset manager, "revenue strategy" reads as revenue management: rate, inventory, IDeaS, Duetto. Kate has confirmed Ignite is explicitly not that.

The risk: a prospect hears "revenue," maps Ignite to a category they already buy, concludes "we have that," and Ignite spends the rest of the meeting arguing it is different from a category it never wanted to be in.

The related risk in "there's nothing like Ignite": with no reference frame, a buyer does not know what Ignite is, who owns the decision, or which budget line it comes out of. No category means no budget. This is the most common way a genuinely novel product stalls, and it is a positioning problem rather than a product problem.

**Resolved into a differentiator (Kate, 2026.08.29).** Kate's sharper cut: **RMS looks at the end of the buying journey, promotion, price and availability.** It optimizes the last mile, after demand exists and after strategy is set. **Ignite works across the whole commercial journey and brings the entire commercial team together.** Different altitude, different job, different time horizon.

Said that way the confusion becomes the opening line rather than a risk. Working phrasing: *your RMS decides what to charge for the room tonight; Ignite decides where the revenue comes from next year and who has to do what to get it.* No argument needed, the buyer places Ignite immediately.

**What this implies about the budget (Marcus's read, needs Kate's confirmation):** RMS is bought by revenue management, a single department with its own budget line. If Ignite spans sales, marketing and revenue at once, no single department owns it, so there is no existing line item to buy it from.

- Upside: Ignite sits above the departmental tools, it is a C-suite or asset-management purchase, and the ceiling is far higher than a software line item.
- Downside: a purchase with no home budget takes longer, needs a sponsor senior enough to spend without precedent, and dies if it gets delegated down to a department head who then judges it against tools in their own category.

This is why Stephen Nicholas (Managing Principal) and Ben Brunt (CIO) at Noble are the right entry points. That altitude treats cross-functional spend as normal.

**Warning on the alignment benefit:** "brings the commercial team together" is an alignment claim, and alignment is easy to agree with and hard to pay for. Institutional buyers discount it as soft. Tie it to money: misalignment produces the gap the brand video opens with, and the $1.3M-$2M in overlooked revenue per asset is what that gap costs. **Alignment is the mechanism, recovered revenue is the benefit.** Never pitch alignment as the benefit itself.

## Step 3 — Value: the proof mechanism and the moat (Kate, 2026.08.29)

**The proof mechanism: not what was completed, but which tasks actually produced a revenue lift.**

Every planning and PM tool tracks completion. Completion is activity theater, which is precisely what the brand video attacks in its opening. Tying task to revenue outcome is a materially different claim and is what makes the rest defensible.

**This is what "were we right, or just lucky?" actually means.** The line Kate wanted used as a hook is not a hook, it is the product thesis. Ignite exists to answer that question with evidence rather than instinct. Treat it as the thesis in all pitch material, not as a piece of copy.

**The moat: "what the best do differently," extracted across portfolios and asset classes.** If an urban-market hotel produces a win, test whether it travels to comparable urban hotels, then promote it into a playbook.

- A consultancy cannot do this. It sees one client at a time.
- BI cannot do this. It has no cross-portfolio view.
- An RMS cannot do this. Wrong altitude entirely.

**This settles the Fuse/multi-property decision from the other direction.** Multi-property is not a feature partners are waiting on, it is the precondition for the pattern engine that makes Ignite worth more than the sum of its dashboards. Below a certain number of properties there is nothing to compare. That is the honest reason a strategic partner commits several assets rather than one, and it belongs in the pitch as the reason, not as an ask.

**Risk on the load-bearing claim:** task-to-revenue attribution is the hardest claim in commercial software. Institutional buyers will push on it, and a four-person team asserting clean causal attribution will be tested. Correlation across a portfolio is defensible; direct causation from a single task is not. Overclaiming once with a Noble or a Crescent costs the relationship. **Needs a decision on exact wording before it appears in any material.**

## Step 5 — Category: **Revenue Growth System** (confirmed by Kate, 2026.08.29)

Candidates considered and rejected: Commercial Operating System, Commercial Strategy Orchestration, Portfolio Commercial Intelligence, Portfolio Value Creation, Portfolio Growth System. **"Commercial" was rejected because it does not resonate with owner/principal buyers like Stephen Nicholas.** Bare "revenue" was rejected as colliding with revenue management, which Ignite sits roughly three levels above.

**Why "Revenue Growth System" works:**

- **It turns the RMS collision into the differentiator.** Revenue management optimizes what you already have; revenue growth creates what you do not. Same root word, opposite job. The buyer sorts it themselves without an argument.
- **It matches the core metric.** The Ignition Score is Revenue Growth × Gross Profit Margin. The category name is the numerator.
- **It matches the domain.** igniteyourgrowth.com already says growth. No rename, and the site headline stops fighting the category.
- **"System" ties to the video's own argument**, Clear's "you fall to the level of your system." Plain enough for an operator, serious enough for a principal.

**"Engine" was ruled out deliberately:** Sales Engine, Marketing Engine and ABM Engine are already components inside Ignite. Naming the whole product an Engine collides with the product's own architecture.

## The attribution claim — RESOLVED (Kate, 2026.08.29)

**Locked wording:**

> **Were we right, or just lucky?**
>
> Ignite tracks which actions show up in revenue across the portfolio, so the pattern is visible even when a single property's result isn't. Then it turns what the best are doing into a playbook the others can run.

**Why the question form is the right answer, and Kate's call not Marcus's.** Marcus drafted three assertion-based options; Kate pointed out the attribution capability simply answers her existing line. That is stronger on both axes at once:

- **It is diligence-proof.** Every assertion about causation has to survive a CFO asking how you know. A question asserts nothing and cannot be falsified, yet lands harder, because the buyer answers it themselves and the honest answer is uncomfortable. The boldest line is also the safest.
- **It does not attack the buyer's tooling choices.** The rejected drafts ("none of them tell you whether it worked") put the buyer on the defensive about decisions they made. Kate's line points instead at their own last good quarter, a win they could not fully explain. Disarming rather than accusatory.
- **It hits the influencer and the buyer with the same sentence.** Asset managers hear the same explanations from commercial teams on repeat with no way to test them. "Were we right, or just lucky?" is that exact doubt, said out loud by Kate first.

**Hard boundary for all material:** never imply Ignite can isolate a single task's revenue contribution at a single property. Correlation across a portfolio is the claim. That is what gets tested, and single-task causation is what breaks.

**Voice note for Maxell (Kate, 2026.08.29): channel Rory Sutherland.** The efficiency-versus-effectiveness argument is Sutherland's central thesis, that optimizing for measurable efficiency destroys the effectiveness nobody measured. Task and project management tools are pure efficiency instruments: they prove work happened, never that it mattered. Ignite is positioned on **efficiency AND effectiveness**. In practice for copy: reframe rather than argue, take the counterintuitive angle, distrust the purely rational optimization case, use vivid concrete contrast over feature lists. Not whimsy, the buyers are institutional.

## The proof story — the 16-to-1 ROAS case (Kate, 2026.08.29, confirmed REAL)

A marketing team reported a **16:1 return on ad spend** and treated it as a win. The campaign was aimed at their **least valuable customer segment**. Against that segment the threshold to actually make the economics work was **22:1**. So 16:1 was a loss wearing a win's clothing, and the team had every incentive to keep believing it.

Move the same spend to a more valuable customer and **8:1 clears the bar**.

**A lower ROAS was the better outcome.** That is the line that stops a principal cold, because it breaks a rule nobody questions.

**Why this is the most valuable single asset in the pitch:**

- **It is "were we right, or just lucky?" with a number attached.** They were neither. They were wrong, confidently, with a good-looking metric as the alibi.
- **It is efficiency versus effectiveness made concrete.** 16:1 is efficient. It was not effective. This is the Sutherland argument in four sentences, using the buyer's own vocabulary.
- **It explains the Ignition Score without a formula.** Revenue Growth x Gross Profit Margin lands as arithmetic. This story shows why the formula exists: "most valuable" means revenue AND profitability together, and without ranking by that, every downstream number is measured against the wrong target.

**What Ignite does with it, per Kate:** (1) gives transparency to the real performance against the right threshold, (2) supplies the critical thinking to move the campaign to a more valuable customer where a lower return actually wins.

Confirmed by Kate as a real case, not an illustration. Client attribution not yet specified; safe to run unattributed until Kate says otherwise.
